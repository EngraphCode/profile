# Elicitation: the interview that produces the writer's own words

This file is loaded at steps 2 and 4. It gives the method, the question bank for building the
episode bank, the deepening probes for the six elements of the shape, and what to do when the
writer says "we", has no example, or cannot name names.

## Method

- **Open questions, one at a time.** "Tell me about a time when" and then nothing. The second
  question waits until the first is answered and captured.
- **Verbatim capture.** The writer types or speaks; you record their words as said, marked as
  verbatim, into the episode bank or the worksheet. Paraphrase is marked as paraphrase. The
  answer will be built from the verbatim lines, so the capture is the raw material, not a note.
- **No vocabulary supplied.** Do not offer words for what they did ("so you took ownership of
  the migration?"). Ask what they did and wait. The words that arrive are theirs and will pass
  the assessor's test; yours would not.
- **Silence.** The particular tends to arrive after a pause. Do not fill the pause with a
  suggestion or a second question.
- **Follow the energy.** Where the writer speeds up, interrupts themselves or laughs is where
  the real episode is. Follow it before returning to the plan.
- **Reflect back their words, not yours.** "You said 'everyone was against it'. Who was
  everyone?"
- **One criterion per sitting of ten to fifteen minutes** for the bank; one episode per sitting
  for the deepening. Stop before the writer tires; thin capture is worse than none.

## Building the episode bank (step 2)

For each criterion, ask three openers and the short follow-ups, and capture one line per
episode: when, where, what the writer decided or did, who else was in it, what came of it. Do
not deepen yet.

The three openers, for any criterion:

1. "Tell me about a time you [the criterion's verb: led, decided, stopped, delivered, changed,
   grew someone, cut]."
2. "When did you last have to [the criterion's verb] and it was hard?"
3. "What is the thing you did at [an employer from their CV] that you would want this panel to
   know about?"

The short follow-ups, used until the line is complete:

- "What did you decide?"
- "Who else was in it?"
- "What happened next?"
- "When was that, roughly, and how big was it?"

### Question bank by criterion

Use the bank's wording as a starting point and rephrase in the writer's own terms once you have
heard how they talk.

**Running live services to a standard.** "What is the worst thing that nearly happened to a
service you ran?" "What did an audit, a test or an assessment find, and what did you do?" "What
control exists because of you?"

**Stopping or redirecting work under way.** "What have you stopped that people had already
built?" "What did you see that they had not, and how did you show it to them?" "What did the
freed time go on?"

**Explaining a technical trade-off to decision-makers.** "When did a board or a director have to
decide something they did not fully understand, and what did you do?" "What question did they
ask that you had not expected?" "What did you refuse to simplify?"

**Setting technical direction.** "What is the biggest technical decision you have made that
others had to live with?" "What did you replace, and who wanted to keep it?" "When did you choose
to buy rather than build, or the reverse, and what did it cost?"

**Growing engineers and the standard they hold.** "Who got better because of something you did,
and what was it?" "How does your team know what good looks like without asking you?" "What gate
did you add that people grumbled about?" "Who now does something you used to do?"

**Disagreeing with a decision.** "When were you sure a decision was wrong? What did you do, and
what did the other side say?" "When were you overruled, and what did you do then?" "When did you
change your own earlier decision?"

**Suppliers and other organisations.** "What have you delivered with another organisation, and
what did they get from it?" "Tell me about working with another organisation where you did not
hold the authority."

**Deciding under a budget constraint.** "What is the largest sum you have been accountable for,
and what did you decide about it?" "When did you move money from one thing to another, and who
argued?" "What did you stop paying for, and who felt it?"

**Leading a function through change.** "Walk me through the function when you arrived and when
you left or now; what changed and which of the changes were your decision?" "What did you build
first, and what did you deliberately not build?" "Which hire or structure do you most regret or
most value?"

**Delivering to a fixed date.** "What was the date, and what happened if you missed it?" "What
did you cut to make it, and who agreed?" "What nearly broke it?"

**Delivering under pressure, learning from failure, stakeholders, inclusion, strategy.**
Use the three openers with the criterion's verb; these rarely need special wording.

## The deepening probes (step 4)

For the chosen episode, elicit each element in the writer's words. Ask the probes in any order
the conversation allows; capture against the element.

### 1. The particular

- "Put me in the room. Where were you, when, what was going on?"
- "How big was it: users, teams, money, time?"
- "What was at stake if nothing changed?"
- "What is the one detail about this that only you would know?"

### 2. The decision or act, and the method

- "What did you decide? Say it as a sentence starting with 'I decided'."
- "What were the options, and which did you not take?"
- "What did you weigh? What tipped it?"
- "What did you do with your own hands, and what did you direct?"
- "How did you do it? Name the method, as you would to someone who wanted to copy it."
- "Why that method and not the obvious one? What had already been tried?"
- For a technical decision: "What was the technical reason: which failure mode, which cost,
  which risk, which limit? What would you have said to someone who called it a preference?"

### 3. The others

- "Who else was in it? Name their role."
- "What did they need from you, and how did you know?"
- "What did they say, as near to their words as you can?"
- "What were they worried about, and were they right?"
- "What was their best case, at its strongest, and what did you do to test it?"
- "What did you change because of what they said?"
- "Who disagreed, and what happened to the disagreement?"
- "If they read your answer, what would they say you had left out?"

### 4. The cost, and the obstacles

- "What did it cost: time, money, goodwill, something that slipped?"
- "What went wrong on the way?"
- "What did you give up to do it?"
- For an obstacles question: "What stood in the way, one thing at a time? Something in the
  system, someone who objected, money or time you did not have? For each: how did you get past
  it, and what did it cost to?"

### 5. The outcome

- "What happened, in a form a sceptic could check?"
- "What number would they ask for, and what is it?"
- "What is the outcome of the kind this criterion is about?" (Name the kind: a service result,
  a change in a person, a decision made.)
- "When did the outcome show, and does it still hold?"
- For a decision-makers question: "What would the decision have been without you?"

### 6. The reflection and the system move

- "What worked, and what would you refine if you did it again?"
- "What would you do differently, and in what order?"
- For a disagreement: "When did you decide to stop pushing, and how did you judge that? If you
  lost, what did you do next?"
- "How was the result better for your part in it?"
- "What exists now because of this that did not exist before?"
- "What cannot happen again, and what stops it?"
- "Who does this now without you?"
- "If you left tomorrow, what of this stays?"
- "What did the team start doing differently, and is it still doing it?"
- "What did you write down, automate or hand over?"

### For a development question, in particular

- "How did you know this person needed something? Who else told you, and did they agree?"
- "What did you do that was planned rather than a reaction to the moment?"
- "Why that method, and why not just more conversations?"
- "What changed for them: a skill, a role, a promotion, staying when they might have left?"
- "What changed in the work: fewer defects, faster reviews, something they now own?"

### The attribution question

Once per episode, after the six elements: "If the other people in the room read this, what would
they say you did?" Record the answer in the ledger as the attribution class for the central claim
(see [truth-and-attribution.md](truth-and-attribution.md)). The writer's answer to this question
is the one that survives interview.

## Targeted interviewing for a gap

When an answer already exists and the diagnosis in
[upgrade-conversation.md](upgrade-conversation.md) names a gap, the interview narrows to that
gap: one question per turn, chosen from the probes above for the element that is missing, the
reply captured verbatim and laid into the paragraph the gap lives in, then reflected back for the
writer to read aloud before the next question. The method does not change; only the selection
does. Two rules bite harder here than in the bank interview: ask one question, not a list, and
when the writer cannot supply the particular, ask once more from a different angle and then
accept the gap rather than offer a plausible sentence.

## Situations

**The writer says "we" throughout.** Ask, for each "we", who did which part. Often the writer
decided and is being modest; sometimes the team decided and the writer carried it out. Both are
fine in an answer when stated; "we" alone is not. Record the split in the ledger.

**The writer has no episode for a criterion.** Say so plainly and early, in step 3, and give the
honest options: the nearest true episode, written without stretching, which will score about
three; or a short candid answer. A writer with less time at the current employer looks across
the whole career, including voluntary and academic work. What never works is dressing an
episode of a different kind in the criterion's words.

**The episode is confidential.** Keep the act, the counterpart's role and the outcome; remove
names, client identities and sensitive figures. "A finance director" and "a regulated client"
are enough. Where a figure is sensitive, give its form ("a six-figure contract") and record the
real figure in the ledger only.

**The writer overclaims.** Do not correct them; ask the attribution question and the probe "what
did you do with your own hands, and what did you direct?" and let their own answer settle the
verb. If the overclaim persists into the draft, name the ledger entry that does not support it
and ask them to choose the verb.

**The writer underclaims.** The same probes. Many writers describe a decision they made as
"helped with". Ask who decided; if the answer is "I did", the verb is "decided".

**Harvesting from artefacts.** The writer's CV, old appraisals, commit history, calendar and
notebooks are prompts for memory, not sources of sentences. Read them to ask better questions;
do not lift their phrasing into the answer. The one place a source's sentences are laid in is
the from-sources mode's scaffold, tagged, for the writer to confirm or replace; the answer still
comes from the writer.

**The writer wants to start from a template answer.** Templates produce the one-star shape. Start
from the interview; the shape comes in step 5.
