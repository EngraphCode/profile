# The shape of a top answer

This file is loaded at step 5. It gives the six-element shape in depth, the per-sentence test,
openers and closers, how to handle limits, and worked examples of one-, three- and five-star
answers to the same question. The examples are invented for illustration and describe no real
organisation or person.

## The six elements

An answer that scores five carries six things, usually in this order, in about 250 words.

1. **The particular.** The first sentence places the reader: where, when, what was at stake, at
   what scale. "In March 2023 our booking service for 40 libraries was failing every Saturday
   morning" is a particular. "I have extensive experience of improving reliability" is not. The
   particular is the sentence only this writer could have written.

2. **The decision or act, and the method.** What the writer did, in the first person, with what
   they weighed. The verb matches the evidence: decided, argued for, built, chose, stopped, hired.
   The alternative not taken is often the most telling clause: "rather than add capacity, I...".
   When the question asks how, the method is named and the reason for choosing it is given: "I
   put the analyst's join of the survey and the case records on the screen rather than present
   the survey, because the team had heard the complaints for a year and discounted them". "I
   explained it clearly" and "I raised my concerns"
   name no method and score as general terms. For a technical question the reasoning is
   technical and named: a failure mode, a cost that compounds, a risk, a limit of scale; never
   "I preferred" or "I was the senior engineer".

3. **The others.** The other people in the question, as people the writer read and responded
   to: what they needed, said, asked, doubted or resisted, and what the writer did in response.
   The finance lead who asked what the money would buy; the developer who said a replay would
   tell him nothing, and was half right; the housing officer who heard what residents said
   first; the supplier who refused. This is the element most often missing from
   otherwise strong answers, and it is the one assessors name when they withhold the fifth star.

4. **The cost, and the obstacles.** What it took, what was given up, what went wrong on the way.
   Honest cost is the mark of a real episode and the thing an invented one lacks. When the
   question asks about obstacles, each obstacle is a different kind of thing that resisted (a
   debt in the system, a person or group who objected, money or time that was not there), named,
   with how the writer got past it, one sentence each. An obstacle the writer "overruled" in a
   clause is a decision, not an obstacle; the assessor wants to see the resistance and the route
   through it.

5. **The outcome.** A fact the reader could check, of the kind the criterion counts, in the form
   it would be reported: "incidents fell from six a month to none in the following quarter", "the
   accessibility audit passed with two minor findings", "she now runs the on-call rota". A figure
   that looks rounded needs its form: "in the following quarter", "across forty branches".

6. **The reflection and the system move.** The last sentences. First, one honest judgement about
   the episode: what worked, what the writer would refine, when they chose to stop pushing, what
   their part changed in the result. This is a judgement about this case, specific
   enough that it could be wrong; it is not a moral. Then what persists: what the writer left in
   place, as an act with a trace: a check that runs on every release, a runbook that is used, a
   person who now owns the thing, a way of deciding the team adopted, a review point that catches
   the next mistake earlier. Never "I always think about prevention".

Three stars is the presence of elements one, two and five told in general terms. Five stars is
the presence of all six with their particulars: the others read and responded to, the method
named and chosen, the outcome in its counted kind, the judgement, and what persists. One and two
stars are the absence of element one.

## The per-sentence test

For every sentence: what does the assessor believe after it that they did not believe before?
If nothing, cut it. If something, but at the wrong altitude (mechanism where the question wanted
judgement; a feeling where it wanted an act), rewrite it at the right one. A sentence that passes
usually contains a verb the writer did and a noun the reader can picture.

## Paragraph jobs

One job per paragraph. A workable allocation for 250 words:

| Paragraph | Job                                        | Share |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----- |
| 1         | The particular and what was at stake       | 20%   |
| 2         | The decision, the counterpart and the cost | 40%   |
| 3         | The outcome                                | 20%   |
| 4         | The reflection and what persists           | 20%   |

Two paragraphs can do the job in 150 words. Five paragraphs in 250 words means one of them is
context the reader did not need.

## Openers

The first sentence is the particular. These openers lose the reader before the particular
arrives, and the script in this skill flags some of them:

- "In my current role as X, I am responsible for..." (a job description, not an event)
- "I have extensive experience of..." (an attitude)
- "Throughout my career I have always..." (a disposition)
- The question's own words: "A time I stopped a piece of work my team had invested in was..."
- A contrast device ("Not the obvious fix. The right one.") more than once across a set of
  answers; it reads as a device.

## Closers

The last sentences are the reflection and the system move. These closers cost a star:

- A moral: "This taught me the importance of communication."
- A tell: "This demonstrates my ability to lead under pressure."
- A restatement of the outcome already given.
- A belief about oneself: "I always ensure that lessons are learned."

A reflection is not a moral. "The alert was the easy part; what I would do sooner next time is
ask finance what they could live with before I designed the fix" is a judgement about this case,
specific enough to be wrong, and it earns. "This taught me the value of involving finance early"
is the same thought as a moral, and it costs. The difference is whether the sentence could only
have been written by someone who was there.

## Terms and acronyms

Each answer is scored on its own. Define every product, team, system and acronym on first use in
every answer, even if another answer defined it. "Our catalogue API, the public interface to
the stock data" costs eight words and keeps the reader. An undefined capitalised phrase reads
as copied from a document the reader has not seen.

## Numbers

A number the reader can check is worth a paragraph of adjectives. Give it in the form it was
published or measured, with its period: "99.95% availability across the 2020 calendar year".
Round figures invite doubt; "no outages" needs "in the following quarter" or it reads as a
flourish.
A number with no source in the ledger does not go in.

## Attribution beside the claim

"I decided to stop the rebuild; the director agreed; the team built the integration" is three
claims with three owners, and a reader trusts all three. "We moved to the integration" is one claim
with no owner, and a reader trusts none of it. Put the credit next to the claim it belongs to,
and keep what the writer did distinct from what the writer influenced and what the writer saw.

## Limits

Treat the limit as a constraint on the final text, not as a target while drafting. Draft to the
shape; then, if over, cut by altitude:

1. Context the reader did not need to believe the particular.
2. The second example, if there are two.
3. Adjectives and intensifiers.
4. A clause of mechanism in the decision paragraph.

Never the counterpart, the outcome or what persists. If the answer cannot fit with all
six elements, the episode is too large for the box, and a smaller episode with all six beats a
large one with four.

Characters and words are different constraints. A form that counts characters usually counts
spaces; 1,500 characters is roughly 250 words of ordinary prose. Read the form's own counter
before submitting.

## Worked examples

All three answer the same invented question for an invented organisation:

> Tell us about a time you improved the reliability of a service your team ran. What did you
> change, and what came of it?

### One star

> Reliability is something I care deeply about. In my current role I am responsible for ensuring
> that our services are reliable and available to users at all times. I have introduced a number
> of improvements, including better monitoring and more robust processes, and I always make sure
> that lessons are learned when things go wrong. As a result our services are now much more
> reliable and our users are happier. I believe this demonstrates my commitment to quality and
> my ability to drive improvements across a team.

No place, no date, no scale, no decision, no person, no figure. Every sentence could be anyone's.
The closing sentence is a tell.

### Three stars

> In 2023 the online booking service for our forty branch libraries was failing most Saturday
> mornings, when demand peaked. I led the investigation and found that a nightly data import was
> locking the bookings table for up to twenty minutes. I rescheduled the import to run in
> smaller batches overnight and added an alert on lock duration. The Saturday failures stopped.
> Over the next three months we had no booking outages, and complaints to branches about the
> service fell to almost none.

A real episode, the writer's own actions, an outcome. No other person appears; the cost is absent;
the thinking stops at the fix.

### Five stars

> In 2023 the online booking service for our forty branch libraries was failing most Saturday
> mornings, when demand peaked, and branch staff had started keeping paper lists. I led the
> investigation and found that a nightly data import was locking the bookings table for up to
> twenty minutes. The simplest fix was more database capacity; the finance lead, who had just
> refused a hosting increase, asked me what the money would buy and whether it would happen again.
> I could not promise it would not, so I chose the fix that answered her question rather than the
> one that answered mine: I rescheduled the import to run in small batches, added an alert on
> lock duration, and spent the two days it cost rather than the money. Saturday failures stopped:
> no booking outages in the following quarter, and the paper lists went away. What I would do
> sooner next time is ask her what she could live with before designing anything; I lost a week
> arguing for capacity I did not need. The part that lasted was not the fix. I wrote the
> lock-duration check into the deployment checks, so any future job that holds a lock too long
> fails in testing rather than on a Saturday, and I paired with the engineer who ran the import
> until she owned the alert and its runbook. She has handled the two lock warnings since without
> me.

The same episode. The finance lead appears, and her question changes the decision; the method is
chosen for a stated reason; the cost is two days and a lost week; the outcome is in the counted
kind; the reflection is a judgement that could be wrong; the last sentences are acts with a trace:
a check that runs on every change, a person who now owns it.

### Disagreeing and losing

> In 2022 our architecture group chose to move the case-management service to an event-driven
> design before the team had run anything event-driven in production. I disagreed on one ground:
> the service's hardest bugs were ordering bugs, and events would make ordering a distributed
> problem we had no tooling to see. I wrote the strongest case for the move that I could, took it
> to the group with a two-day spike that reproduced one of our ordering bugs on the new design,
> and asked for a tracing tool as a condition. The group kept the decision and funded the tracing.
> I committed: I led the first migration myself so the ordering tests were written by the person
> most worried about them. The migration shipped with two ordering defects caught in testing and
> none in production in its first year. I judged that once the group had seen the spike and still
> chosen the design, more pushing would cost me the next argument; the tracing condition was the
> part worth winning. The spike-before-objection pattern is now how the group hears a dissent.

The writer lost and the answer still scores five: the disagreement stands on a named technical
reason, the opposing case is engaged on its strongest ground with evidence, the dissent is open,
the commitment is real and personal, the judgement about when to stop is stated, and the practice
persists.

### A people question, with and without the counterpart

> Tell us about a time you had to give unwelcome news to the people it affected. How did they
> take it, and what did you do with that?

Without: "When the budget was cut I communicated the decision clearly and transparently to the
team and made sure everyone understood the rationale. Morale was maintained and delivery
continued." No person. Three stars at most, and a reader who wants the "how did they take it"
part marks it absent.

With: "When the platform budget was cut by fifteen per cent I told the contractor first, in
person, with six weeks' notice; he asked whether the work would come back, and I said not this
year, which was the truth. I told the housing office manager before the finance circular reached
her; she asked whether the dashboard was the first of several things to go, and I showed her the
ranked list of what would be cut next, which she had not expected to be allowed to see. Monthly
reporting cost her two days a month of hand-collation, which I had underestimated; we automated
the export in the spring. What I would change is the order: I should have asked the housing
office what they used the dashboard for before deciding it was the thing to drop. The ranked
list is now a standing page the team maintains and the director reads each quarter." The people
are there, in role not name; each asks something and the writer answers it; the cost to one of
them is stated and was underestimated; the reflection is a judgement; the last sentence is what
persists.
