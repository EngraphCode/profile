# The rubric, and how to read a question as the assessor will

This file is loaded at step 1 of the pipeline. It explains how an anonymised sift is scored, gives
the default one-to-five rubric to use when the employer's own scheme is not published, shows how to
infer the marking scheme from the question, and lists criterion by criterion what a three-star
and a five-star answer contain and which kind of outcome each criterion counts.

## How an anonymised sift is scored

Platforms such as Applied, and processes such as the UK Civil Service's Success Profiles sift,
share a shape. Check the employer's own description; where it differs, the employer's description
wins.

- The applicant's name, CV and other identifying details are hidden from the people scoring.
- Answers are separated from one another and presented by question, so an assessor reads a batch
  of answers to the same question, often in a shuffled order, without the applicant's other
  answers alongside.
- Each answer is scored by more than one assessor against a marking scheme written for that
  question, usually by the person who wrote the question. The scheme is not published; the
  criteria are.
- The scale is commonly one to five (Applied uses stars) or one to seven (Civil Service
  behaviours). The anchors below are for a five-point scale; map them if the employer's scale
  differs.
- A moderation step may look at all of an applicant's answers together, after the per-question
  scores, to settle borderlines and to check consistency. Repetition across answers is visible
  there even though each assessor saw one answer at a time.
- The CV is usually read only for applicants whose answers scored well.

Two consequences shape every answer. Each answer must stand alone, with every term defined,
because its reader has nothing else. And the reader is tired: they have read many answers to this
question in one sitting and are primed to reward the particular and to mark down anything that
could describe anyone.

## The default rubric

Use this when the employer publishes nothing beyond the criteria. Read it as a ladder: each rung
contains the one below.

| Score | Name          | What the answer contains                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ----- | ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1     | Empty         | Generic, or off the question. No particular situation. Could be anyone's experience, or reads as produced by a machine. Attitude statements in place of events.                                                                                                                         |
| 2     | Thin          | A real situation is gestured at, but the writer's own part is unclear ("we"), a part of the question is missing, or no outcome is given.                                                                                                                                              |
| 3     | Competent     | A specific, real example. The writer's own actions and decisions are clear. Every part of the question is answered. An outcome is stated.                                                                                                                                              |
| 4     | Strong        | Everything in 3, and the other people in the situation appear as people who said or did something; the cost or difficulty is honest; the outcome is checkable and of the kind the criterion counts; the writer's judgement is visible in what they weighed.                              |
| 5     | Excellent     | Everything in 4, and something the writer left in place: a check that runs, a runbook that is used, a person who now owns the thing, a way of deciding the team adopted, each as an act with a trace rather than a belief about themselves.          |

Read across the ladder, the step from three to five is made of five things, and none of them is
more words: the others in the situation as people the writer read and responded to; the method,
named and chosen for a reason, where the question asks how; the outcome in the counted kind; an
honest judgement about what worked and what the writer would refine; and what persists. A
three-star answer typically has the example and the outcome and tells the rest in general
terms; a five-star answer has the particulars of each. An answer can be short and score five.

Two things are worth holding separately. First, reflection is scored, not decoration: an answer
with no judgement about the episode is capped below the top, however good the rest. Second, each
question's one-star rung tends to name a specific bad behaviour, not only vagueness: for a
question about stopping work, pressing on to protect what was spent; for a question about a
failure, blame; for a question about growing someone, a routine described with nobody different
at the end of it. The opposite of that behaviour is what the question's author most wants to
see.

## What one star looks like, by kind of question

The author of a question usually has a bad behaviour in mind that the question is designed to
expose. Write it down in step 1; the elicitation then probes for its opposite.

| Kind of question | The behaviour screened out at one star | Its opposite, which five rewards |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Explaining a trade-off to the people who decide it | No sense of what the deciders needed; the technical point either dumbed down or dumped on them | A method chosen for that audience, the hard part left in, the decision better for the writer's part |
| Disagreeing with a decision | A story about personalities; the writer gave way without saying why, or carried on after the decision | Technical reasoning, the other side's best case engaged, evidence sought, open disagreement, commitment if overruled |
| Running a service that failed its users | A post-mortem that blames a supplier or a predecessor; a fix with no change to how the service is run | The first days owned, the users told, a cause found, and a control that has run on every change since |
| Growing someone | A routine described, nobody different at the end of it; "regular one-to-ones" | A need identified from more than one source, a chosen method with its reason, a change in the person and in the work |
| Stopping or redirecting work under way | Pressing on to protect what was spent, or stopping by fiat with no one brought along | The evidence that turned the decision, the people who had built it read and answered, what the freed capacity did |
| Introducing a practice against resistance | The practice mandated; the resisters cast as the problem | The resistance understood as information, the practice adapted because of it, in use without the writer |
| Deciding under a budget cut | The cut passed down evenly; nothing chosen | A protected thing and a dropped thing, each with its reason, explained to the people affected before they heard it elsewhere |
| Delivering under pressure | Heroics, or a missed deadline explained | A trade-off made and owned, the next deadline made calmer |
| Working with stakeholders | "Engaged stakeholders"; no one named by role, nothing changed by them | A stakeholder's need read and acted on, a decision they now own too |
| Learning from failure | Blame, or a failure that was not the writer's | The writer's own judgement examined, a changed practice with a trace |

## When the writer was overruled

A question about disagreement does not require the writer to have won. An answer in which the
writer made the technical case, lost, and then committed fully once they had lost and
carried it through, with a sentence on how they judged when to stop pushing, can
score five.
What scores one is silence, or an argument carried on after the decision.

## When the question says "led"

A question about work the writer led is testing ownership. The central claim must be in the
Decided class of [truth-and-attribution.md](truth-and-attribution.md): the writer set the
direction and carried it. An Influenced or Contributed claim, stated honestly, caps the answer
at three; stated dishonestly it fails the interview.

## Reading the marking scheme from the question

The scheme is unpublished, but the question's author wrote both, and the question carries the
scheme's shape. For each question, write down:

1. **The parts.** Most questions have two or three: "tell us about a time", "how did you go
   about it", "what came of it", "what changed for them". The scheme almost always has a line
   per part.
   An answer that drops a part is capped, however good the rest.
2. **The criterion.** Match the question's nouns to the published criteria. "A service you were
   responsible for" maps to the live-services criterion; "someone you managed" maps to the
   developing-people criterion. Where two criteria fit, the first clause of the question usually
   names the one that governs.
3. **The counterpart.** Who else is in the question: a supplier, a user, the sponsor, whoever
   objected, the people on the team. The scheme for a question about people will reward evidence of
   what those people said and did and what the writer did in response.
4. **The counted outcome.** What kind of result the criterion is about. A service question
   counts a service result (incidents down, a recovery time met, an audit passed), not a launch.
   A development question counts what changed for a person, not a process introduced. A
   decision question counts the decision made and what followed, not the writer's method alone.
5. **The method and the judgement five rewards.** When the question asks how, the scheme
   rewards a named method and the reason for choosing it: the data joined and shown rather than
   summarised, a trial run rather than a paper, a written plan rather than a conversation.
   And it rewards an honest judgement about the episode: what worked, what the writer would
   refine, when they let go.
6. **The behaviour screened out at one star.** Every question has a bad answer its author has
   seen too often. Name it (see the table above) and probe for its opposite.
7. **What the author probably hoped would persist.** For a service question, a check
   that runs on every change. For a question about change, the practice in use without the
   writer. For a people question, a person who now does the thing. Write your guess down; it
   tells the writer what the last paragraph is for. Not every question's scheme requires
   something that persists; where it does not, the honest judgement about the episode carries
   the last paragraph and the persisting change is one sentence, not the close.

Show this reading to the writer before any interviewing. They may know the organisation, the
author or the role better than the question lets on.

## Criterion by criterion

The first group are criteria common to senior engineering and digital roles. The second group
are common to most roles. For each: what three contains, what five adds, the counterpart, and
the counted outcome.

### Engineering and technical leadership criteria

| Criterion | Three contains | Five adds | Counterpart | Counted outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Running live services to a standard | A real service, its scale, the standard met, the writer's part in meeting it | A failure mode named and the check that closes it; an audit or assessment passed; the check running on every release | The auditor, the user, whoever is paged | An availability, accessibility or assurance result, not a launch |
| Stopping or redirecting work under way | What was stopped, on what evidence, what the capacity did instead | The people who had invested in it, read and answered; the sunk cost named and set aside openly; a review point the team now uses | Those who had built it, the sponsor | What the freed capacity delivered, with a figure |
| Explaining a technical trade-off to decision-makers | The decision, the technical point it turned on, how the writer explained it | A question the deciders asked and what the writer did with it; the hard part left in, not smoothed; a way of deciding that persists | The deciders, as people who spoke | The decision made, and what it led to |
| Setting technical direction (build or buy, platform, architecture) | A direction chosen, its reasons, what it enabled | What it cost, who disagreed, how it was brought in step by step, what runs on it now | The people who held the previous shape | What the platform carries now that it could not |
| Growing engineers and the standard they hold | A person coached, a standard set and how it is held | How the standard is held without the writer (the gate, the review, the pairing); one person's progress over time, by role; a person who now holds the standard | An engineer, by role | A change in a person, and in the work's defects, pace or reviewability |
| Disagreeing with a decision | The decision, the disagreement, what the writer did, the result | The other side's case stated fairly; the point the writer gave ground on; how the disagreement was turned into something the group could test; how the team disagrees now | Whoever held the decision | The decision changed or upheld, and what followed either way |
| Working with suppliers and other organisations | A real collaboration, the writer's part, what was delivered or shared | What the writer did without holding the authority; a norm set for how the organisation works with others | The partner, the supplier, the other organisation | Something delivered or adopted outside the organisation |
| Deciding under a budget constraint | The size and the lines, a decision that moved or cut money, a reason for it | A trade-off made and its consequence stated honestly; the people affected told first; a control that stops the overspend recurring | Finance, a supplier, a director, the people affected | A figure: saved, redirected, held within tolerance |
| Leading a function through change | A dated span, the scale, decisions the writer took that shaped the function | What the writer changed as the function grew, and why then; the resistance understood rather than overcome; what the function does now without the writer | The engineers and managers | The function's shape and capability, with a figure |
| Delivering to a fixed date | The date, what was at stake, the plan, what was delivered | What was cut to make the date and who agreed the cut; the risk that nearly broke it and the control that caught it; what the next date inherited | The sponsor, the team, whoever took the cut | The thing delivered on the date, and the next one delivered calmer |

## What assessors say they mark down

These come from employers' own published advice to applicants and from fresh reads in testing.

- Answers that could describe anyone: no place, no date, no scale, no name of a thing.
- Answers written in "we" throughout, where the writer's own decision cannot be found.
- Attitude in place of event: "I always make sure that", "I am passionate about".
- The question's own words handed back as if they were an answer.
- A part of the question missing.
- A result that is not a result of the kind asked for.
- A polished sentence where the evidence should be. One such sentence per answer is a style;
  three is a substitute for substance.
- Terms the reader cannot know, left undefined.
- Signs of machine authorship: uniform sentence length, lists of three everywhere, abstractions
  that fit any employer, a closing moral.

## When the employer publishes its own scheme

Use it in place of the default rubric and map its anchors to the pipeline's gates. If it names
behaviours or values (for example, "collaborative", "curious"), treat each as a criterion with a
counted outcome of its own kind, and ask the writer for the episode where that behaviour was a
decision rather than a disposition.
