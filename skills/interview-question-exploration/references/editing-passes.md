# Editing a sift answer in your own voice

This file is loaded at steps 5 and 6. It gives the editorial method for a person writing about
their own work for an assessor: writing in one's own voice, the five pitfalls and their repairs,
showing rather than justifying, the three editing altitudes, the per-sentence test, and the
check before submission. The agent uses it to point; the writer uses it to rewrite.

## Your own voice

The test of voice is simple: would you say this sentence, aloud, to a colleague who was there?
If not, it is not yours yet. Sift answers fail this test in two directions: the applicant who
writes in a borrowed formal register ("I was responsible for the facilitation of"), and the
applicant who lets a machine or a template supply the sentences. Both read as nobody. The cure
is the same: say it, capture it, then tidy it.

A person who knows what they did and why sounds confident without claiming to be. The confidence
is in the verbs and the particulars, not in adjectives about oneself.

Where the writer has supplied samples of their own writing, their voice profile
([voice.md](voice.md), [assets/voice-profile.md](../assets/voice-profile.md)) gives the test
evidence: the rhythm, the connectives, the verbs and the words that are theirs, with their own
lines quoted. The line pass points at a sentence that breaks the profile; the writer decides.

## Show, do not justify

Describe what you did and what it made possible. Do not explain why you are the sort of person
who would do it. "I stopped the release and spent two days on the import job" shows judgement.
"I take a careful, risk-aware approach to releases" claims it, and a reader who sees the claim
without the act believes neither.

Two things that look like justification are content, and assessors score them. First, the
reason for choosing a method. When the question asks "how did you", the how is the answer, and
the reason the writer chose that how over the obvious one is part of it: "I asked the developer
who objected to run the first replay with me, rather than present the gate, because a mandate
would have proved nothing to him" is method and reason, both content. What goes is the reason
about oneself: "because I believe people need to see things for themselves". Second, an honest
judgement about the
episode: what worked, what the writer would refine, when they stopped pushing. That is
reflection, and it earns; it is distinguished from a moral by being specific to this case and
capable of being wrong.

## The five pitfalls

These are the five ways a true answer goes wrong at the sentence level. Each has a repair.

### 1. Justification

Explaining why, as though the reader needs convincing of your motives.

| Justifying                                                           | Showing                                                                                 |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "I introduced automated checks because quality is important to me"   | "I introduced automated checks; defects found after release fell from nine to two a quarter" |
| "I believed it was essential to involve the team, so I ran workshops" | "I ran three workshops; the team chose the structure"                                   |

### 2. Passive and descriptive framing

The act is there but no one did it.

| Passive                                                      | Agentic                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| "A new on-call rota was put in place"                        | "I wrote the on-call rota and ran it for the first month"        |
| "My role involved ensuring the migration ran smoothly"        | "I ran the migration in three stages over six weeks"              |
| "This has included working with stakeholders"                | "I met the finance lead weekly until the budget was agreed"       |

### 3. Tell sentences

A sentence that summarises what the paragraph already showed, or announces the quality it was
meant to demonstrate. "This demonstrates my ability to lead under pressure." "This shows the
importance of communication." Cut them; the paragraph either showed it or it did not. A
reflection is not a tell: "I would ask finance first next time; I lost a week arguing for
capacity I did not need" is a judgement about this case, and it stays.

### 4. Hedging

A true claim softened until it says nothing, often as modesty. "I helped to support the delivery
of" is three hedges on one act. Say the act with its class: "I ran the delivery" if you did, "I
built the reporting for it" if that was your part. Attribution beside the claim replaces the
hedge: "I proposed it; the head of service decided."

### 5. Unclear abstractions

Words that could mean anything: stakeholders, solutions, outcomes, improvements, best practice,
engagement. Replace each with the thing: who, what, which number. "I engaged stakeholders" is
nothing; "I met the two ward managers who had refused the rota and changed the handover time
they objected to" is an act with people in it.

## Level shows, it is never argued

Scale is stated as fact: the size of the team, the money, the users, the span of time. It is
never argued through an org chart, a reporting line or a comparison with other roles. "I was
accountable for a team of twelve and a budget of £400,000" is a fact a reader can use.
"Although my title was X, in practice I operated at Y level" is an argument a reader distrusts.
A smaller fact, stated, beats a larger claim, argued.

## Verbs match evidence

The strength of the verb never exceeds the strength of the evidence in the ledger. "Transformed"
needs a before and after a reader could check. "Led" needs a decision the writer made. "Built"
needs the writer's hands on it. When the evidence is weaker than the verb, change the verb, not
the evidence.

## Repetition hides in nouns

"I built the pipeline and built the team's confidence in it." "The new process... the process...
the process." Read the draft aloud before any reader does; the ear catches the echo the eye
accepts. Across answers, the script flags repeated sentences; the ear catches repeated devices.

## Each answer stands alone

Define every term on first use in every answer. The assessor of answer three did not read answer
one. A product name, a team name, an internal programme, an acronym: each gets its eight words
of definition every time.

## The per-sentence test

For every sentence, ask what the assessor believes after it that they did not before. Nothing
new: cut. New but at the wrong altitude (mechanism where the question wanted judgement, a
feeling where it wanted an act): rewrite at the right altitude. This test, applied once to the
whole draft, removes most of what a limit would otherwise force out.

## The three editing altitudes

Edit from large decisions to small, one altitude per pass, never two in one pass. Polishing
sentences inside a structure that has not earned its shape is wasted work.

**Developmental.** Does the answer do the question's job? Read it once without writing, then
summarise in one sentence what the answer actually is; compare that with the question's parts
and the criterion; name the structural change to make first. Is the counterpart present as a
person? Is the outcome of the counted kind? Is what persists an act? Leave sentences
alone unless a sentence signals a structural fault. The agent's output here is a short letter
naming the change; the writer makes it.

**Line.** Clarity first, rhythm second. Concrete for abstract. Vary sentence length. Remove
redundancy within a sentence and across paragraphs. One polished line per answer at most; the
rest is evidence. A fix that loses the writer's voice is the wrong fix. The agent proposes cuts
and order and points at pitfalls; the writer rewrites.

**Proof.** The last pass. Every name, role, date, figure, title and product against the ledger.
Consistency of capitals, hyphens and numerals. Nothing in the answer that the ledger does not
hold as confirmed. The agent may correct a figure to what the ledger holds and flags every
ambiguity; it never silently changes a fact.

## What the agent may change

At the developmental altitude: nothing in the text; a letter naming the structural change.
At the line altitude: proposed cuts and reorderings, shown as proposals; pointers at pitfalls
with the repair pattern, not the repaired sentence. At the proof altitude: corrections to match
the ledger, each named. At no altitude does the agent add a claim, a figure or a sentence of its
own to the answer.

## The check before submission

1. Does this sound like someone who knows exactly what they did and why?
2. Would the writer say every sentence aloud to a colleague who was there?
3. Is every sentence showing or claiming, never explaining or justifying?
4. Are the people in the question present as people the writer read and responded to?
5. Is the method named, and the reason for it given, where the question asked how?
6. Does the reader finish with the writer's honest judgement of the episode and with what the
   writer changed so that it lasts?

Six yeses, and the answer goes to the assessor read. Any no is a return to the altitude where
the fault lives.
