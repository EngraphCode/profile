## The voice in one line

Proposed, for the writer to rewrite: long, plain sentences, one event each, their clauses joined
with "and" or "because"; openers that set the time; the decision placed with whoever made it;
no hedges, no self-description, and the consequence delivered last.

## Habits, with their own lines as evidence

### Rhythm

Sentences run long, 13 to 40 words, median 26, most carrying two clauses joined by "and" or a
"because" clause; paragraphs of three or four sentences; no lists. Length varies within a
paragraph: a 13-word sentence follows a 31-word one. "I took the previous year's pre-sale
traffic and replayed it against the new engine in a two-day spike test, three weeks before the
sale. The first run fell over at about a quarter of the expected load." [S2]

### Openings and connectives

Opens with a time or a condition ("In the autumn of 2021", "Once that was settled", "On the
day"), or with "I", "We" or "The". Joins with "and", "because", "so that", "until"; no
signposting; one colon, which delivers the consequence of the sentence before it. "The replay
test became a standing gate: nothing changes on the sales path now without the previous
pre-sale's traffic being replayed against it first." [S2]

### Verbs and stance

Own acts in plain past-tense verbs: "argued", "took on", "took ... and replayed". Claims without
hedging, and places the decision with whoever made it rather than claiming it. "I" for the
argument, the choice and the test; "we" for the team's finding and fixing. Other people by role,
drily, with what they decided. "I had argued earlier in the year for using a hosted provider for
exactly this reason, and the head of digital had kept the decision to build our own engine so
that the member journey stayed ours end to end." [S2]

### Vocabulary

The words for their world: "spike", "load-testing workstream", "replay", "session store",
"bottleneck", "pre-sale", "the sales path", "standing gate", "fell over". No words about
themselves, no "stakeholders", "ensure", "leverage" or "best practice". British usage
("autumn"); no spelling that settles British or American appears in the sample. No
contractions. Approximate figures in words ("about forty times", "about a quarter", "one and a
half times", "two-day", "three weeks"); the headline figure in digits ("40,000 tickets"). "Our
members' sales produce about forty times the normal daily peak in the first hour." [S2]

### Devices and texture

One dry aside that does the reasoning's work rather than decorating: "I wanted the test written
by someone who expected it to fail." [S2] No metaphor beyond "fell over"; no questions; no
parentheses, dashes or semicolons; a colon once. Paragraphs end on the consequence: "...until it
held at one and a half times the expected peak." [S2] "...without the previous pre-sale's
traffic being replayed against it first." [S2]

### Register range

One register in the sample: plain, first person, evidential, addressed to a stranger, which is
the register a sift answer wants. No chatty, formal or spoken sample exists yet; the interview
captures will show how the writer speaks. "On the day the pre-sale sold 40,000 tickets in the
first hour with no outage." [S2]

## Keep and lose

Open: the writer answers "Which of these habits do you want kept in the answers?" and "Which
would you rather lose?" The agent's proposals, for them to strike or keep:

Habits the writer may want kept in the answers:

- The time-stamped opener that places the reader at once.
- The decision placed beside whoever made it.
- The dry aside that carries a reason.
- Figures in the form they were measured, approximate ones in words.

Habits the writer may rather lose, which the line pass would point at:

- "We" with nobody named in it ("We found the bottleneck"); one instance, so an observation.

Things they never do in the sample, which the agent never introduces in a join (provisional on
232 words):

- Semicolons, dashes, parentheses.
- Contractions.
- Lists.
- Sentences about themselves ("I am someone who").
- A closing moral.

## The check

1. Does this sound like A. B., as the lines above sound?
2. Would A. B. say every sentence aloud to a colleague who was there?
3. Is every sentence showing or claiming, never explaining or justifying?
4. Would a reader of all four answers hear one person?
5. Do the measured figures for the finished answers sit inside A. B.'s range (sentences of 13
   to 40 words, median about 26; "I" about 2.6 per 100 words; no semicolons, dashes or
   parentheses), and where they do not, was it a choice?
