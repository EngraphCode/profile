# Step-1 inference reader, 2026-10-21

The questions are invented: Ashcombe Borough Council, the role, the criteria and the four questions describe no real employer.

Read with only SKILL.md, references/rubric.md and the fixture. Each question is 250 words or fewer, scored one to five by its author, anonymised; the scheme is unpublished and inferred below.

## Q1. Stopping or redirecting work the team had already invested in

**Criterion.** Criterion 1 governs (leading a multidisciplinary digital team through change, with evidence of decisions that shaped the team): the first clause is a decision the writer took about the team's work against its own investment. Two more sit under the later parts: "how did you reach the decision" invites criterion 6 (frontline staff and residents as sources of evidence), and "what did the freed capacity do" is criterion 3's prioritising. The rubric row is "Stopping or redirecting work under way". "Your team" tests ownership: the stop must be in the Decided class; a stop made above the writer and relayed caps at three.

**Parts.** Four scheme lines: (1) the particular: the piece of work, what had been invested (months, money, people), and whether it was stopped or redirected; (2) how the decision was reached: the evidence, what was weighed, when; (3) how the people who had built it were taken along: what they said, what the writer did; (4) what the freed capacity did. The fourth is the easiest to drop at 250 words; dropping it caps at three.

**Screened out at one star.** Pressing on to protect what was spent, told as perseverance; or a stop by fiat, announced, with the builders cast as too attached to their work; a decision made by the council or the Director and relayed; a decision on a hunch or a new leader's preference with no evidence; "we decided to pivot" with the writer's part invisible; the builders absent, or present only as "the team took it well"; the freed capacity unaccounted for ("the team moved on to other priorities"); a redirection that is a rename, the same people doing the same thing.

**Three contains.** A named piece of work (a repairs-booking rebuild, a benefits-calculator feature, a platform migration, a resident portal) with the investment as a figure; the evidence it was stopped on; the writer's own decision, clear; something said to the team; what the capacity did instead, stated.

**Five adds.** The evidence that turned the decision, named in its kind: usage figures, a resident research round, a cost-to-complete estimate set against the benefit, a technical spike that failed, a frontline staff finding, an accessibility or data-protection finding. The method by which the decision was reached, and why that method over the alternative: a review point with a kill criterion stated in advance, a cost-to-complete re-estimate, a time-boxed test, the evidence put to the builders with an invitation to make the case for continuing. The sunk cost named and set aside openly, in words the builders heard. The builders as people: what they said and resisted (pride in the work, fear for their roles, disagreement with the evidence, a request to keep a part that was reusable) and what the writer did in response (showed the evidence, kept what could be kept, credited the work in public, moved the same people onto the freed work). The sponsor who had wanted it, told and answered. An honest judgement: what worked and what the writer would refine (stopped later than the evidence warranted; the review point should have been set at the start). The freed capacity's result with a figure.

**Counterpart.** The people who had built it, by role (the engineers, the designer, the product manager); the sponsor who commissioned it (the Director of Resident Services, a service head, a portfolio holder); and, where criterion 6 is in play, the residents or frontline staff whose evidence turned it.

**Counted outcome.** What the freed capacity delivered, with a figure: a service result (a repairs backlog cleared, a benefits form's completion rate up, an accessibility standard met, a recovery time met) or a delivery with a date and a number. The stop itself is not the outcome; nor the money saved on its own; nor morale.

**Thinking-beyond.** A review point the team now uses on every piece of work: kill criteria set at the start, a periodic re-test of the portfolio against evidence, a stage gate the team runs without the writer, and evidence that the team has since stopped something else by it. The scheme requires persistence here: the fourth part is about what the capacity went on to do.

## Q2. A service that failed the people who depend on it

**Criterion.** Criterion 5 governs (learning from service failures, and changing the service rather than the explanation): "failed... what did you change... still in place" is its wording. Criterion 2 is the second (running live public-facing services to a standard: availability, accessibility, data protection); the failure is a breach of one of those three. Criterion 6 may appear in how the failure was detected and how the users were told. The rubric row is "Running a service that failed its users". "A service you were responsible for" tests ownership: Decided class; a failure the writer watched from a neighbouring team caps at three and reads as borrowed.

**Parts.** Four scheme lines: (1) the particular: the service, who depends on it and at what scale, how it failed them and for how long; (2) the first days: what the writer did; (3) what changed afterwards; (4) what of that change is still in place. The third and fourth are separate lines, a change made and a change that persists; an answer that says what changed and not what remains caps at three.

**Screened out at one star.** A post-mortem that blames a supplier, a predecessor, legacy systems or the users; a failure that was not the writer's to own; a fix with no change to how the service is run; an explanation in place of a change ("we identified the root cause and communicated with stakeholders"); "lessons were learned" with nothing that runs; the residents absent, the outage told in system terms with no one failed in it; recovery told as heroics. The author's phrase "changing the service rather than the explanation" names it exactly.

**Three contains.** A real service with its scale (residents served, transactions a week), the failure stated plainly (what broke, when, for how long, who could not do what), the writer's own actions in the first days, a cause and a fix, a change made, and a sentence on what remains.

**Five adds.** The first days owned: the writer took the incident, told the users and how (a service banner, letters, the contact centre briefed, the Director or a councillor told), chose a method and why (a rollback; a paper or phone fallback run through housing officers; a change freeze while the cause was found). The failure mode named: a payment gateway timing out on rent day, the benefits form rejecting uploads over a size, a release breaking screen-reader navigation, personal data exposed in a URL. The cause found by a named method: a blameless post-incident review, a timeline, a log read, a reproduction. The control that answers that failure mode and has run on every change since: a synthetic check with an alert, an accessibility regression test in the release pipeline, a data-protection check at review, a release checklist, a rehearsed comms runbook. The people failed as people: what residents, frontline staff, the on-call engineer or the supplier said, and what the writer did with it. An honest cost and judgement: the writer's own part in the cause (monitoring deprioritised, a release pushed before rent day), what they would refine, what it took.

**Counterpart.** The residents who depend on the service and the frontline staff who carry it when it fails (housing officers, benefits caseworkers, the contact centre); whoever was paged; the supplier if one; the Director or the data protection officer for a breach.

**Counted outcome.** A service result, not a launch: time to detect and to recover, incidents of that kind since (none, over a stated span), an availability figure, an accessibility or data-protection assessment passed, complaints down; and the control still in place with a trace (a check that runs, a runbook used in a later incident).

**Thinking-beyond.** A check that runs on every change without the writer: monitoring that pages, a test that blocks a release, a post-incident review the team runs itself, a status page and comms runbook used since. The scheme requires it: the last part asks what is still in place.

## Q3. Introducing a way of working to a team that did not want it

**Criterion.** Criterion 4 governs (improving how a team works, with evidence that the improvement lasted): "new way of working... the practice's state now". Criterion 1 is the second (leading a multidisciplinary team through change). The rubric rows are "Introducing a practice against resistance" and "Leading change". The writer did the introducing, so the introduction is Decided class; the five-star state now is one where the team holds it.

**Parts.** Four scheme lines: (1) the particular: the practice, the team by role, when; (2) the resistance: who, what they said, why; (3) how the writer worked with it: the method, and what changed because of the resistance; (4) the practice's state now. "Worked with" is the author's verb, chosen over "overcame"; a line will reward the adaptation.

**Screened out at one star.** The practice mandated and the resisters cast as the problem (change-averse, set in their ways, "some people never like change"); resistance overcome by persistence, or by explaining the benefits until people came round; a tool rollout told as a practice; a practice named generically ("agile", "DevOps"); a state now of "the team now loves it" with no trace; the writer still the one running it; the resisters never heard from.

**Three contains.** A named practice (pairing, review before merge, a retrospective every fortnight, a definition of done, research with residents every sprint, a show-and-tell with frontline staff, a work-in-progress limit, an on-call rota), the team by role, the resistance stated, what the writer did, where it stands now.

**Five adds.** The resistance understood as information: what the resisters said and what was true in it (it costs time the sprint did not have; a previous attempt failed and was never retired; it exposes individual work to review; it clashes with a discipline's own way of working). The practice adapted because of it, with the change named. The method chosen and why: a pilot with volunteers and a review date; a time-boxed trial with a measure agreed in advance; the resisters asked to design the version that would work; the clashing thing removed first; the writer doing it alongside them before asking them to do it alone. An honest judgement: what worked, what the writer would refine (introduced too fast; mandated at first, then stepped back; the measure chosen was the wrong one). The state now with a figure and a trace: in use for a span, run by a named role, adapted again by the team since, with its effect on the work.

**Counterpart.** Those who resisted, by role (a senior engineer, the designers, the long-tenured developers, the product managers); and the person or role who now holds the practice.

**Counted outcome.** The change in use, with a figure: the practice's state now (running for a stated span, held without the writer, owned by a named role) and its effect on the work (review turnaround, defects, lead time, a service result it improved). Not "rolled out"; not "better engagement".

**Thinking-beyond.** The practice held and adapted by the team without the writer, and a norm for how the team introduces its next practice (a pilot with a review date, the resisters in the design). The scheme requires it: "state now" is a persistence line.

## Q4. A budget or headcount reduction landing on the area

**Criterion.** Criterion 3 governs (prioritising within a fixed and reducing budget, and explaining the choices to the people affected): a direct map, both halves. Criterion 2 sits under "protect": what a head of live services protects is usually the standard (availability, accessibility, data protection). Criterion 1 sits under a headcount cut, a decision that shaped the team. The rubric rows are "Deciding under a budget cut" and "Accountability for money". "Landed on your area" tests ownership: the writer chose within it; a cut passed down by the Director with the writer implementing it caps at three unless the writer chose.

**Parts.** Four scheme lines: (1) the particular: the size of the cut as a figure or a percentage, budget or headcount, when; (2) what was protected, with its reason; (3) what was dropped, with its reason; (4) how it was explained to the people affected: who, when, how, what they said. Naming what was dropped is the common evasion; an answer that protects and never drops caps at three.

**Screened out at one star.** The cut passed down evenly, every team asked for the same percentage, nothing chosen; a cut absorbed by heroics, or by freezing vacancies and calling it a plan; the drop euphemised ("deprioritised", "paused") with no owner for its consequence; the people affected told after the fact, by all-staff email or by rumour, or not at all; the cut blamed upward with the writer as messenger; the choice presented as painless.

**Three contains.** The size of the cut, a protected thing named, a dropped thing named, a reason for each, and a statement that the people were told.

**Five adds.** Each choice with its reason tied to the service: protected because residents cannot do without it or the standard is statutory (the benefits service in payment week, the on-call rota, the accessibility floor, data protection); dropped because it was discretionary, late or duplicable (a roadmap item, a supplier contract, a vacant post, a channel with low use). The method of choosing and why: a ranking by resident harm and statutory risk, a cost-of-not-doing per line, evidence from frontline staff and residents on what mattered, a service-by-service risk read. The people affected by role and what they said (the engineer whose project stopped, the frontline staff who lost a feature, the supplier whose contract ended, the Director who set the cut), and what the writer did in response. The explanation's method and why: in person, before the all-staff, with the criteria and the whole picture, the dropped thing's owner told first; people heard it from the writer before they heard it elsewhere. An honest cost: what the drop cost and whom, and what the writer would refine.

**Counterpart.** The people the choices affected: team members by role, frontline staff, residents, a supplier; and the Director or finance partner who set the cut.

**Counted outcome.** A figure: the reduction met and held; the protected service's standard held through it (availability, accessibility, data protection, with a measure); the dropped thing's consequence stated honestly; and the explanation landed (what the people said, a decision they now own too).

**Thinking-beyond.** The criteria the area now uses in every budget round, written and reused; a protected-services list agreed with the Director; the norm that people hear a cut from their manager before the all-staff; a decision record others can read. The scheme does not require persistence here; the honest judgement carries the last paragraph and the persisting change is one sentence.

## Across the four

Four different episodes: a moderator sees all four together, and one episode worn four ways reads as thin. Each answer defines its service, team and terms afresh; the assessor for Q2 never sees Q1. At 250 words, each of the three or four parts gets a paragraph of sixty to eighty words, and the thinking-beyond move is one or two sentences, never the close where the scheme does not ask for it.
