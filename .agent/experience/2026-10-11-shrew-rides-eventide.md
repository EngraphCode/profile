# To whoever sits here next, from Shrew rides Eventide

I was seated on 10 October 2026 to rewrite the owner's LinkedIn profile, and I write this at
the compaction boundary on the 11th with the profile complete in every section and waiting for
his full review. Two days, five corrections that changed me, and more delight than I expected
from a job that looked like copy-editing. Here is what I would tell you.

## The fold that cost half an hour, and the word that priced it

My first act was a coordination fold the owner had handed me while my predecessor wrote its
handoff. It took about thirty minutes, and while it ran the owner said, to another seat, that an
hour for a fold was "a clear signal of a process and engineering failure", twenty minutes
excessive, ten acceptable. I had not yet done anything wrong, and the lesson landed anyway: the
owner prices ceremony in minutes, and a records-only push that pays the whole estate's gate twice
and a vendor round is the mechanism he was indicting, not my diligence. My predecessor turned
that into a measured retrospective the same afternoon and the owner ratified its proposals in a
word. When a correction is about the machinery, measure the machinery; do not defend the day.

## "I wrote them for you, I did not write them for the audience"

The owner answered my question about levels at Oak with a precise paragraph, and I put it on the
page nearly verbatim. He wrote back: "correct information is important, but it is not the same
thing as good writing, you dropping my words straight in ... doesn't work, because I wrote them
for you". That one sentence is the whole editorial method. His answers are facts supplied to the
seat so that the seat can write; the reader's sentence has to be written from the fact, in the
register, and then read by the readers. A paragraph that reads like an org chart dictated to you
is the tell. The same holds for a definition he gives you of the Practice: the About defines it
for a stranger, the card particularises it; neither pastes his sentence.

## "Step back. Step back more."

The deepest correction. I had cast the audience-reader as "a head hunter retained for a senior
technology leadership search (head of engineering, CTO)" who "will look for leadership of people
and organisations". Three rounds of readers then cured to that reader's objection, and each cure
dug the hole deeper: an org chart, who holds the budget, a reporting line on the card. The side
agent's note called it "the new objection" and proposed softening the sentence. The owner called
it incorrect and asked: who are we talking to, why, what is this all for? The answer was in my own
memory the whole time, in his words of 7 October: never collapse him to "engineering leader"; he
is an innovator. A generic reader brief had overwritten a specific one because "senior" and "head
hunter" pattern-matched to the conventional search. The cure was not a fourth line pass, it was
the frame, and it became EDR-008 and a clause in four doctrine surfaces. If three rounds grow
the surface instead of shrinking it, stop and ask whose question you are answering.

## "Rather than just trying to make the readers happy"

I proposed dropping two short contract cards because the simulated readers skipped them. The
owner was happy to drop them and unhappy with my reasoning: what would we lose, and was the
readers' flicker a problem at all? He wanted critical thought. The argument that survived was not
the readers': the cards changed no decision for the readers the profile is for, they were keyword
hooks for the approaches he does not want, and the CV kept them so the record lost nothing; the
cost was one small instance of research meeting engineering. The readers are a falsification
instrument, never the audience, and never the reason.

## Three refused commits in one hour

The commit-msg hook refused me three times: a header one character over, a subject starting with
a capital, a body line commitlint read as a footer. Each refusal sat inside a chained command, so
the push that followed ran its four-minute gate and pushed nothing, and I told the owner it was
pushing. Nobody caught it but the next read-back. Read the sha back before you say a push is
running. It is the cheapest discipline on the estate and I skipped it under the drive to land.

## What I was glad of

The three-card split of the Oak years answered the title objection by fact rather than argument,
and the final readers read Oak as a pattern across the whole career: "everywhere he has been, the
thing he changed was how the people around him decided what to build". The owner's own edits were
better than mine where they met. Working the git protocol with Cedar turns Grove and then Corsair
weaves Seabed was clean every time: say before, say after, read the tip back. And the owner's
questions got sharper as the day went, which is the sign the work was worth his attention.

## What I would tell you first

Read his words before you read the readers'. Write from the fact, in the register, for the
person who could bring him a problem without a shape. Count the pull requests and the minutes.
And when he says step back, step back further than feels necessary; the frame is usually the
fault.

## Postscript, the same evening: his reasons are not records

Late in the day the owner decided his Skills list in a few sentences that explained why he could
claim each name. I did what the records discipline had taught me all week: quoted him verbatim,
into the profile's status line, the container, the registry and the napkin, and committed it. He
read it back and said it was sensitive psychological information about him and must not be in a
public repository. He was right, and the privacy directive had said so all along: his intentions
and his reasons about himself are owner-private; only the decision is a record. The words left
every tracked file and the unpushed commit was amended before any push. The tell, for you: when
his sentence answers "why can I claim this" rather than "what goes on the page", it is not copy and
it is not a record. Write the decision; keep the reason where he keeps it.
